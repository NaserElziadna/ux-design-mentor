# The Mom Test - Rob Fitzpatrick

How to ask good questions and validate ideas through real user conversations, not biased feedback.

## Core Problem: The Mom Test

**Definition:** When you ask your mom if your business idea is good, she'll say yes because she loves you. Not because your idea is actually good.

The same applies to most people: they're bad at giving honest feedback in interviews.

**Why people lie in feedback:**
- They want to be nice
- They don't want to hurt your feelings
- They don't want to seem mean
- They assume you know more than you do
- They want to help you succeed

**The solution:** Stop asking people what they think. Watch what they do.

## Key Principle: Actions Over Words

People will tell you what they think you want to hear.

**Their actions reveal truth:**
- Are they using your product?
- Are they paying for it?
- Are they recommending it?
- Are they coming back to it?
- Are they spending time on it?

**Feedback hierarchy (best to worst):**
1. Observed behavior (watching them use it)
2. Real-world behavior (what they actually did in past)
3. Stated opinion (what they say they like)
4. Polite agreement (yes, that sounds cool!)

## How to Ask Good Questions

### Rule 1: Ask About the Past, Not the Future

**Bad:** "Would you use a design feedback tool?"
(They'll say yes, but won't actually use it)

**Good:** "When was the last time you needed design feedback?"
(Reveals if they actually need this)

**Bad:** "Would you pay $20/month for this?"
(Hypothetical; people lie about hypotheticals)

**Good:** "How much did you spend on design tools last year?"
(Real behavior reveals actual willingness)

### Rule 2: Ask Open-Ended Questions

**Bad:** "Do you think this design is confusing?"
(Leads them to answer yes or no)

**Good:** "What did you think of that design?"
(Reveals their actual reaction)

**Bad:** "Is your current tool slow?"
(Leads them to agree)

**Good:** "Tell me about your experience with your current tool"
(Reveals real experience)

### Rule 3: Dig Into Specific Details

**Bad:** "I'm building a design critique tool. Is that valuable?"
(Too abstract; they'll agree)

**Good:** "The last time you needed feedback on a design, what was the process? Who did you ask? How long did it take? Were you happy with the feedback?"
(Specific, reveals real experience)

### Rule 4: Avoid Leading Questions

**Bad:** "Don't you think designers spend too much time on feedback?"
(Implies answer you want to hear)

**Good:** "Tell me about the last time you got design feedback. How did it go?"
(Neutral, open-ended)

**Bad:** "Isn't feedback the biggest challenge in design?"
(Implies your assumption)

**Good:** "What's the biggest challenge you face in design work?"
(Let them tell you)

### Rule 5: Acknowledge Their Stake in Lying

People know they shouldn't say negative things. Acknowledge this.

**Don't:** "Be honest, this design is kind of ugly right?"
(Still leading)

**Do:** "I know it's hard to give negative feedback. Tell me what bothers you about the design."
(Acknowledges social difficulty, invites honesty)

## Red Flags in Feedback

These signal the person is being polite, not honest:

- **Excessive positivity** - "Oh that's great!" (vague, positive, done)
- **Hypothetical commitment** - "I would definitely use that" (future, not real)
- **Hypothetical payment** - "I'd pay $20 for that" (hypothetical value)
- **Compliments without criticism** - No product is perfect
- **Polite brush-off** - "Sounds interesting" (not really interested)
- **Asking when it launches** (engagement without real intent)
- **Vagueness** - "That's cool" (doesn't engage with specifics)

**What you want to hear:**
- Specific past behavior
- Concrete problems they've faced
- Specific costs they've incurred
- Signs of real interest/urgency
- Questions about specifics
- Honest criticisms
- Concrete next steps they commit to

## Good Conversation Starters

### Find the Right Person

Don't ask your friend. Ask someone who actually experiences the problem.

**Bad:** "Do you think students would like a study app?"
(Friend guessing)

**Good:** "You told me you struggle with studying. Tell me about that."
(Person actually experiencing problem)

### Start with General Conversation

**Don't:** Jump to your idea
**Do:** Learn about their life/work first

Example conversation:
1. "Tell me about your role as a designer"
2. "What's your typical day like?"
3. "What's the most frustrating part?"
4. (They mention problem naturally)
5. "Tell me more about that..."
6. (Now introduce your idea if relevant)

### Avoid Selling

**Don't:** "We're building something to solve your problem. What do you think?"
(You're now selling, not learning)

**Do:** "I noticed you mentioned feedback is slow. Tell me more about that."
(You're learning, not selling)

### Keep Talking Ratio Low

You should listen more than talk.

**Bad ratio:** You talk 70%, they talk 30%
**Good ratio:** You talk 30%, they talk 70%

If they're talking more, you're learning more.

## Validating Ideas

### The Commitment Test

Don't ask if they like the idea. Ask if they'll do something about it.

**Weak commitment:** "That's interesting"
**Medium commitment:** "Let me know when it launches, I want to try it"
**Strong commitment:** Concrete next step they schedule/do

Examples of strong commitment:
- "I want to pay for a beta version"
- "Can I be a design partner?" (with specific time commitment)
- "When can I start using it?"
- Schedule next conversation for specific future date

### The Three Calls to Action

After talking to someone, ask:
1. **Can I interview them again?** (Are they willing to continue talking?)
2. **Can I show them a prototype?** (Are they willing to see your work?)
3. **Can I ask for an introduction?** (Are they willing to refer you?)

If they say no to all three, they weren't that interested.

## Research Framework

### Phase 1: Problem Validation
Talk to people with the problem:
- "Tell me about a time you faced this problem"
- "How often does this happen?"
- "How did you solve it?"
- "What's the cost of this problem?"

Goal: Confirm people actually care about this problem

### Phase 2: Solution Validation
Show them your idea:
- "Here's how I'm thinking about solving this..."
- "What do you think?"
- "What would make this actually useful to you?"
- "What would you change?"

Goal: Confirm your solution addresses their actual problem

### Phase 3: Willingness to Pay
Understand economic viability:
- "How much does this problem cost you?"
- "What have you spent on similar solutions?"
- "Would you pay for this? How much?"

Listen to actions, not words.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### Mistake 1: Asking Friends and Family
They will lie to be nice.

**Fix:** Find people who actually experience the problem

### Mistake 2: Pitching Your Idea
You'll bias the conversation toward agreement.

**Fix:** Ask about their experience first, introduce idea naturally

### Mistake 3: Asking Hypothetical Questions
People lie about hypotheticals.

**Fix:** Ask about past behavior and real experiences

### Mistake 4: Only Asking for Feedback
You'll get polite agreement.

**Fix:** Ask for commitment (next conversation, intro, beta access)

### Mistake 5: Not Taking Notes
You'll forget important details.

**Fix:** Record conversations (with permission) or take detailed notes

### Mistake 6: Only Talking to Enthusiasts
Early adopters aren't representative.

**Fix:** Talk to regular people, not just those excited about your space

## Key Validation Questions

Build your own mom test around these:

**Problem validation:**
- "Tell me about the last time you faced [problem]"
- "How often does this happen?"
- "What's the impact when it happens?"
- "How much time/money does it cost you?"

**Solution validation:**
- "Have you tried anything else?"
- "What worked? What didn't?"
- "What would perfect look like?"
- "What's one thing you'd change about my idea?"

**Commitment validation:**
- "Would you be willing to [specific action]?"
- "Can we schedule time [specific date]?"
- "Can I show you a prototype next week?"
- "Can you introduce me to someone else facing this?"

## Summary: The Mom Test Framework

1. **Talk to people with the problem** (not your mom or friends)
2. **Ask about their past behavior** (not hypothetical future)
3. **Listen for commitment** (not polite agreement)
4. **Watch what they do** (not what they say)
5. **Ask for next steps** (get them to do something)
6. **Document everything** (remember what you learned)
7. **Iterate** (continuously talk to users, not just once)

## Creating Your Validation Plan

**Choose 3-5 target customers:** Real people with the problem

**Write 5-10 questions** covering:
- Problem experience (past)
- Current solution
- Desired solution
- Willingness to commit

**Conduct 5-10 conversations** over 2-4 weeks

**Track:**
- What problems came up repeatedly?
- Who showed real commitment?
- What surprised you?
- What would you change?

**Iterate:** Change your solution, ask new questions, test again

This is continuous, not one-time validation.
